In 1866, the ocean developed a problem.

Ships were encountering something enormous. A long, spindle-shaped object, occasionally phosphorescent, far larger and faster than any whale. It appeared in logbooks across the world with eerie consistency: sailors who had never met each other described the same impossible creature, moving through the water at speeds that made seasoned captains question their instruments.

I should note that the word "creature" did a lot of heavy lifting in those early reports. No one quite knew what to call it.

The first credible sighting came on July 20th, 1866, when the steamer *Governor Higginson* encountered a moving mass five miles off the coast of Australia. Captain Baker initially mistook it for an uncharted sandbank -- until two columns of water shot hissing into the air, a hundred and fifty feet high. Sandbanks, as a rule, do not exhale.

Three days later, the *Columbus* spotted the same thing in the Pacific. The distance between the two sightings: more than seven hundred nautical leagues. Whatever this creature was, it could cross oceans the way other animals crossed rivers.

Two weeks after that, the *Helvetia* and the *Shannon*, sailing the Atlantic between America and Europe, spotted it simultaneously and triangulated its length at over three hundred and fifty feet. For context, the largest whales ever recorded barely reached sixty yards.

The reports kept coming. The *Pereire* observed it. The *Etna* collided with it. Officers aboard the French frigate *Normandie* filed an official report. Commodore Fitz-James surveyed it from the deck of the *Lord Clyde*. Each account confirmed the others, and the serious nations -- England, America, Germany -- began to pay attention.

The creature became a sensation. It was satirized in newspapers, sung about in cafes, lampooned on stage. Cartoonists drew every sea monster imaginable, from Moby Dick to the kraken. Ancient authorities were dusted off -- Aristotle, Pliny, the Norwegian tales of Bishop Pontoppidan -- as if the accumulated weight of legend might somehow explain what modern science could not.

Then came the great debate. Scientists took sides, and for six months the learned institutions of the world went to war over the question of the monster. The Smithsonian, the Royal Academy of Berlin, the British Association, the Geographical Institution of Brazil -- all weighed in. The popular press, never one to miss an opportunity, mocked the believers ruthlessly. One particularly devastating satirical piece seemed to settle the matter entirely. Wit, as so often happens, defeated science.

The monster was declared dead by ridicule.

It should have stayed that way. But the ocean, as I would later learn, does not read newspapers.

On March 5th, 1867, the *Moravian*, carrying 237 passengers home from Canada, struck something in the North Atlantic at thirteen knots. There was no reef marked on any chart for that stretch of water. The officers rushed to the stern and found nothing but a churning eddy, as if something large had just dived beneath the surface. When the *Moravian* was examined in dry dock, they discovered a broken keel.

A submerged rock? An enormous wreck? No one could say, and the incident might have been forgotten -- except that three weeks later, something worse happened.

On April 13th, the *Scotia*, pride of the Cunard line, was cutting through calm seas at thirteen and a half knots when her hull received a sharp, precise blow. Not the grinding scrape of a reef or collision -- something pointed and penetrating.

No one aboard would have noticed at all if the ship's carpenter hadn't come running up to the bridge shouting that they were sinking. Captain Anderson calmed the passengers -- the *Scotia* had seven watertight compartments, and the damage was survivable -- then went below to assess. Seawater was pouring into the fifth compartment with alarming force. Anderson ordered the engines cut. Divers found a hole nearly two yards across in the ship's bottom.

The *Scotia* limped into port three days late, causing considerable anxiety in Liverpool. When she was put in dry dock, the engineers could scarcely believe what they found. Two and a half yards below the waterline was a clean triangular gash, as precise as if it had been cut by a machine. The iron plating -- nearly three and a half centimeters thick -- had been pierced with enormous force, and whatever had done the piercing had then pulled itself free -- a reverse withdrawal that no one could explain.

That was the detail that changed everything. Not just the impact, but the withdrawal. A reef doesn't pull itself free. A rock doesn't retreat. Whatever had struck the *Scotia* had done so deliberately, and then moved on.

After that, every unexplained shipwreck got blamed on the monster. And there were plenty to choose from -- of the three thousand ships whose loss Lloyd's recorded each year, some two hundred simply vanished without explanation. Fair or not, the creature now bore the weight of all that mystery.

Transatlantic travel had become genuinely dangerous, and the public had lost patience with scientific caution. The demand was simple and unanimous: whatever this thing was, it had to be found and destroyed.
